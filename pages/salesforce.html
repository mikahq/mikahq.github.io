Improving member–provider matching with AI

As a student user researcher for Salesforce, I led mixed-methods research into healthcare referral workflows — uncovering where AI could reduce administrative burden without eroding the trust that makes care feel human. Our recommendations shaped how AI gets embedded in contact center tools across the member, provider, and agent journey.

Role User Researcher
Timeline 4 months, 2025
Team 4 UX Researchers
Methods Landscape review, survey (n=16), IDIs (n=6), affinity mapping
Tools MAXQDA, R, Sheets
Problem

When a patient needs a specialist, a referral shouldn't take weeks. But it often does — because the systems meant to connect members, providers, and contact center agents weren't designed to talk to each other. Fragmented records. Manual verification steps. A phone call that starts over every time it transfers.

Salesforce wanted to understand where AI could help. Not in a vague, futurist sense — they needed concrete answers about specific workflow moments. The complication: providers were already skeptical. Patients were already confused. And the contact center agents caught in the middle were burning out.

Discovery

We started with 20+ sources before talking to anyone — forums where patients vented about referral delays, research on contact center burnout, industry reports on IVR failure rates. The landscape review gave us enough to know what we were walking into before we designed a single survey question.

The survey (n=16) let us compare across all three seats at once: member, provider, agent. What looked like an individual frustration in the patient forum turned out to be a systemic breakdown visible from every angle. Then six in-depth provider interviews gave us the specific, frustrated, sometimes resigned way people described their days.

Approach

I focused my analysis on the provider interviews, which carried the sharpest signal about AI trust. Using MAXQDA to code themes, a pattern emerged that the survey had only gestured at: providers weren't opposed to AI as a concept. They were opposed to what they'd already seen AI do. Specifically — what their patients had experienced, and came back upset about.

"That's what they call it, like a robot and not a real person. Yeah, and they hate that."

We built a swimlane diagram mapping where member, provider, and contact center journeys intersect — and more usefully, where they silently fall apart. The swimlane became the spine of our executive presentation because it made the handoff failures visible in a way that a findings deck couldn't.

Findings

Three things shaped our recommendations.

AI trust has a generational dimension. Older patients came in with prior bad experiences with automated systems — IVRs that misheard them, chatbots that looped. Their distrust wasn't irrational. It was informed. And providers were managing that distrust on top of their own.

Information decays across departments. Patient context — what they need, what they've already tried, what they're afraid of — erodes at every handoff. By the time it reaches the right person, it's incomplete. This is exactly the kind of invisible, structural problem that AI can address backstage, before the patient ever picks up the phone.

Providers see a ceiling, but it isn't zero. The most useful thing a provider said wasn't about the technology. It was about the specific type of question that wastes their staff's time.

"Some of the questions our participants answered… could definitely be answered by AI. Very, very minimal kind of questions like that where it does take time out of staff's day."
Outcome
n=22 Participants across survey + interviews
20+ Sources in landscape review
NDA Full findings restricted

Our recommendations focused on embedding AI in the background: automating verification, surfacing context for agents, streamlining provider matching — so that care feels more connected, efficient, and human. We presented an executive report and the swimlane deliverable to the Salesforce team. Full findings are restricted under NDA.

The people most open to AI weren't the youngest ones in the room. They were the ones most exhausted by repetition.
