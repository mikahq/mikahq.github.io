Teaching science as a social process and combating misinformation

A mixed-methods research and design project asking how K-12 educators might teach students that science is built on peer review, consensus, and collective knowledge-vetting — not just authority. Conducted n=10 educator interviews and developed three prototype concepts as part of a 5-person interdisciplinary team.

Role Project Manager & User Researcher
Timeline 4 months, 2025
Team 5 students: 1 Information Science PhD, 1 Software Engineer, 1 Architecture student, 2 Masters of Information Sciences students
Methods Competitive analysis, interviews (n=10), affinity mapping
Skills Lean agile process, project management, UX design, UX research
Tools MAXQDA, R, Sheets
Problem

Education scholars often say that one reason we have such a problem with misinformation is that K-12 science classrooms are not teaching students to critically evaluate scientific claims they may encounter in online environments. They point to two remedies: teaching students hard fact-checking skills (investigating the source of a claim, "lateral reading"), and teaching students that science is a social process.

With the information that students are taught currently, they may be led to believe that science is a pure and authoritative source of knowledge — if scientists have discovered something, then it must definitely be true. But educational standards aren't building in ideas of scientific consensus, peer review, or publication bias as important factors in evaluating our shared understanding of the world.

Our design question: How might we equip K-12 science educators with accessible methods and resources to teach students that science is a social process built on peer review, consensus, and collective vetting of knowledge claims?

References: Osborne et al. (2022). Science Education in an Age of Misinformation. Stanford University. Brodsky et al. (2021). Improving college students' fact-checking strategies through lateral reading instruction. Cogn. Research 6, 23. McGrew, S. (February 2024). Teaching lateral reading.

Process

We ran a competitive analysis to explore the problem space and identify what already existed before designing anything. Then we conducted ten interviews with K-12 science educators and English language teachers and administrators — understanding their current practices, their challenges, and what "teaching science as a social process" even looks like from inside a classroom.

We visualized pain points and opportunity clusters through affinity mapping, and used MAXQDA to extract themes from the interviews. From that synthesis, we developed three prototype concepts.
