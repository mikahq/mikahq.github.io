Visualizing libraries, books, and their patrons

An individual data visualization project drawing insights from two public datasets: the San Francisco Public Library (2004–2016) and the New York Times list of best books of the 21st century. Also includes a redesign of an existing Hollywood gender gap infographic.

Role Individual — data gathering, visualization, insights
Data sources SF Library (City & County of San Francisco), NYT Best 100 Books
Tools R, Tableau, Excel, Observable Plot
Methods Exploratory data analysis, hypothesis generation, data cleaning
Approach

I gathered the data from the City and County of San Francisco and from the New York Times Best 100 Books from the 21st century. Before doing any analysis, I generated questions into hypotheses — a practice I've since made standard: writing down the goal or takeaway before drafting a visualization guides the format I decide to go with. Data prep and cleaning happened in R, Tableau, Excel, and Observable Plot depending on what the data needed.

Finding — SF Library

The San Francisco Library boasts multiple branches in a densely populated city, with an impressive dataset spanning 2004–2016. While readership varies in other months, the summer is consistently busy — June, July, and August see a massive spike in readership regardless of branch location.

The preference for contact methods in library communications underwent a shift around 2013. Phone, print, and email were neck and neck until email rose ahead drastically. While the majority of people checking out books skewed older (around 65+), they had a more even preference across all methods. This contrasts with patrons under 55, who much prefer email.

Finding — NYT Books

The New York Times came out with a list of the "best books of the 21st century," polling famous authors. Out of the 100 books on the list, 71 were fiction. But when we look at ratings from the general public, non-fiction consistently ranked more highly than fiction — above a 4.0/5.0. People love reading fiction, but they rate non-fiction higher. That's a gap worth sitting with.

I also created an organized category of sub-genres by main genre. Historical books had a good mix of both fiction and non-fiction, but most genres were siloed. Why aren't books — at least in this list — doing genre crossovers? What about genres not mentioned or captured here?

Redesign — Hollywood Gender Gap

For a separate information visualization project, I focused on redesigning an existing visualization that highlights the gender gap in Hollywood. My goal was to make it more clear and engaging while conveying the same information — using a more deliberate color scheme and adding context to make it more accessible to a wider audience.

Lessons learned

Choosing the message of the visual is key. Writing down the goal or takeaway before drafting is crucial — it guides the format.

Understand what information will be left out. When I choose to represent one kind of information or transform the data, there will always be other types of information I may omit for clarity. That's a decision, not an accident — and it should be made consciously.

There's always room for improvement. Not all of the visualizations I create will be as clear as possible, but I strive to incorporate feedback in the re-design.
