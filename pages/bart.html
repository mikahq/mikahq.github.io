A Case of Convenience: Bay Area Rapid Transit

An independent research study into why Bay Area riders simultaneously love and resent BART. Using ethnography, in-depth interviews, and desk research, I surfaced three patterns that explain a transit system's paradox — and proposed redesigned Clipper cards as a community-building intervention.

Role User Researcher
Timeline October 2024
Team Independent
Methods Desk research, IDIs (N=5), ethnography
Tools Otter.ai, Apple Notes transcription, MAXQDA
Skills Research circulation, data visualization, UX research, design
Context

BART is not a system people feel neutral about. Bay Area riders defend it, complain about it, write local op-eds about it, and then ride it the next morning anyway. That contradiction — love and resentment occupying the same commute — is what this project set out to understand.

The system started service in 1972 and covers the North Bay, Peninsula, and San Francisco. After COVID, ridership collapsed — by 2023, fare revenue was covering only 25% of maintenance costs. At the same time, BART is pushing ahead with expansion into San Jose, completing the "transit loop" that's been on the books for decades. A system financially hemorrhaging, building outward. That tension isn't just a policy question. It shapes what riders believe about the institution, and how they use it.

Discovery

I combined three methods: desk research to trace the system's financial and political history, ethnographic observation at stations and on trains to see what the experience actually looks like on the ground, and five in-depth interviews to hear how riders make sense of all of it.

Transcription happened through Apple Notes and Otter.ai, then coding in MAXQDA. I organized quotes by theme afterward.

Findings

Three patterns gave the data shape.

Classification confusion drives disappointment. Riders think of BART differently depending on who they are and where they're going — subway, metro, commuter rail, regional connector. The system functions differently than all of those analogies suggest, which means rider expectations are almost always misaligned. Especially around wait times. BART isn't slow — it's just not what riders mentally modeled it as when they got on.

Transit is social infrastructure, not just transportation. Crowded cars can feel energizing; near-empty ones feel unsafe. Riders who commute with colleagues describe BART as part of their work culture. Usage patterns reflect this — heaviest on Tuesdays, lightest on weekends.

"People in general are thankful to have it. But nonetheless, it's going to be underfunded and neglected and really hard to improve."

The loyalty is transactional. This was the most unexpected finding. Participants could articulate clearly why BART frustrated them — and still ride it every day — because the alternative was worse.

The pattern mirrors how people relate to other under-resourced public institutions. Public radio. Libraries. Community clinics. People stay because they believe in what the thing is supposed to be, even when it may fall short.

Outcome
N=5 In-depth interviews
3 Research methods combined
2 Deliverables: report + card redesign

I delivered both an executive report and an alternative artifact: BART Clipper cards redesigned to build a sense of community around the transit system rather than just processing a transaction. Recommendations included a marketing strategy to realign rider expectations, and a station art program modeled on New York City's subway installations — starting with stations like Oakland and Downtown Berkeley where foot traffic and local identity already overlap.

Remaining questions

Many things. Among them: How do the needs of BART riders genuinely impact their habits? Who used to ride BART, but doesn't now, and why? Who started to use BART after COVID? Why? Who does BART aim to serve, and who are they serving now?

Transactional loyalty is still loyalty — and maybe understanding that distinction is the first step toward designing something that earns a different kind.
